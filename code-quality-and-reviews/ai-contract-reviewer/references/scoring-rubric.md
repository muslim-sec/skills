# Scoring Rubric — Worked Examples

Read this when you're unsure whether a clause is a 4 or a 6. Calibration matters — if every contract gets graded too harshly or too leniently, the dashboard becomes useless.

For each score, here are real examples of clauses (English and Arabic equivalents) and the reasoning that places them there.

---

## Score 1 — Clean, standard, in user's favor

**Example (English):** *"Either party may terminate this Agreement for convenience upon ninety (90) days' prior written notice to the other party."*
- Mutual termination right. Notice period is reasonable. Nothing to negotiate.

**Example (Arabic):** *"يحق لأي من الطرفين إنهاء هذه الاتفاقية لأي سبب وفقاً لتقديره، بموجب إشعار خطي مسبق مدته تسعون (90) يوماً."*
- Same — mutual, reasonable notice.

**Reasoning template:** "Mutual, balanced, market-standard. Sign as-is."

---

## Score 2 — Standard, neutral

**Example:** *"This Agreement shall be governed by the laws of the State of Delaware, without regard to its conflict of laws principles. The state and federal courts located in Wilmington, Delaware shall have exclusive jurisdiction."*
- A reasonable, predictable jurisdiction. Both parties would likely be fine. Not in the user's favor, not against them.

**Reasoning template:** "Standard boilerplate, neither party gains an edge."

---

## Score 3 — Minor friction, normal market

**Example:** *"Payment shall be made within thirty (30) days of receipt of an undisputed invoice."*
- Net 30 is industry standard for B2B. A freelancer might prefer Net 15 but Net 30 isn't a problem.

**Reasoning template:** "Slightly less favorable than ideal but standard practice. Worth knowing, not worth fighting over."

---

## Score 4 — Worth noting but acceptable

**Example:** *"The Company may, in its sole discretion, modify the Employee's job duties and responsibilities."*
- Common in employment contracts. Acceptable but means the user could be reassigned without recourse. Worth being aware of, not a red flag.

**Reasoning template:** "Within normal range but tilts slightly toward the other side. Acceptable unless combined with other concerns."

---

## Score 5 — Notable risk, raise it

**Example:** *"This Agreement shall automatically renew for successive one-year terms unless either party provides written notice of non-renewal at least ninety (90) days prior to the end of the then-current term."*
- 90-day notice is easy to miss. The renewal compounds the lock-in. Negotiable to 30 days or convert to month-to-month after the first year.

**Reasoning template:** "Real risk the user might miss this. Worth specifically negotiating a shorter notice window or removing auto-renewal."

---

## Score 6 — Real risk, should negotiate

**Example:** *"Customer shall indemnify and hold harmless Provider from any and all claims, damages, losses, and expenses arising out of or related to Customer's use of the Service."*
- One-sided indemnity with no cap and a very broad "related to" trigger. Should be (a) mutual, (b) capped at a reasonable amount, (c) limited to third-party claims.

**Reasoning template:** "Materially favors the other side. Negotiate to mutual + capped + scoped to third-party IP/breach claims. If they refuse, that's a real warning sign."

---

## Score 7 — Serious risk, push back hard

**Example:** *"All inventions, ideas, works, and discoveries conceived, developed, or made by the Employee during the term of employment, whether or not during working hours and whether or not on Company premises, shall be the sole and exclusive property of the Company."*
- Standard IP-assignment clauses normally cover work *related to the company's business* and *using company resources*. This clause grabs everything — including personal side projects on the user's own time. Push for a clear carve-out for personal projects unrelated to the company's business.

**Reasoning template:** "Significantly over-broad. Push back hard for narrower scope. If they won't agree to a carve-out for personal/unrelated work, this is a serious problem."

---

## Score 8 — Severe risk, get advice

**Example:** *"In the event of any breach by Contractor, Client shall be entitled to liquidated damages in the amount of fifty thousand dollars ($50,000) per occurrence, payable immediately."*
- On a contract worth a fraction of $50k, this is punitive rather than a reasonable estimate of harm. Likely unenforceable in many jurisdictions but signals the other party will try to enforce it anyway, and the user would have to litigate. Get a lawyer before signing.

**Reasoning template:** "Severe and likely punitive. Won't necessarily be enforceable but the fact that it's in the contract signals bad faith. Strongly recommend legal review before signing."

---

## Score 9 — Extreme risk, deal-breaker as written

**Example:** *"Employee waives any and all right to participate in any class action, collective action, or representative action against the Company, and any disputes shall be resolved exclusively through binding arbitration with an arbitrator selected by the Company at the Company's discretion."*
- Stacks class-action waiver + forced arbitration + the *other party picks the arbitrator*. Effectively eliminates the user's ability to seek redress for almost any wrong. The arbitrator-selection part alone is over the line.

**Reasoning template:** "As written, this clause is structurally abusive. Either strike or fundamentally rewrite. If they insist, walk away — this signals the entire relationship will be unfair."

---

## Score 10 — Don't sign, abusive or likely unenforceable

**Example:** *"Contractor agrees that for a period of ten (10) years following the termination of this Agreement, regardless of cause, Contractor shall not, directly or indirectly, engage in any business activity anywhere in the world that competes with any business activity of Client, its affiliates, or any of its current or future portfolio companies."*
- Global, decade-long non-compete covering future and unrelated businesses. Unenforceable in most jurisdictions, but the user shouldn't sign something explicitly designed to intimidate. Score 10 even though they probably couldn't enforce it.

**Reasoning template:** "Abusive on its face. Likely unenforceable but signing means tacit agreement and an intimidation tool against the user. Refuse outright."

---

## Calibration check

After scoring a contract, look at your distribution:

- **All 1s and 2s?** Either it's a genuinely fair contract or you're being too lenient. Re-read with the user's interests specifically in mind.
- **All 7s and 8s?** Either the contract is genuinely bad or you're being too harsh on standard boilerplate. Re-check what's actually market-standard.
- **Suspiciously even distribution (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8...)?** You may be rationalizing scores to "spread them out." Each score must be earned on its own merits.

A typical fair B2B contract has most clauses at 1–3, a few at 4–5 worth flagging, and maybe one 6 worth negotiating. A typical predatory contract has its 1s and 2s in the boilerplate and clusters of 6–8 in the operative clauses (payment, IP, termination, indemnity).

---

## When the user is on the *other* side

Most of the time the user is the weaker party (employee, freelancer, customer, tenant). But sometimes they're the stronger one (employer, client, vendor, landlord). When that's the case:

- The same clauses get **different scores**. A liability cap of 12 months' fees, which is generous when the user is the customer, becomes risky when the user is the vendor.
- **Don't change the underlying analysis — change the perspective.** Always score from the user's seat.
- Tell the user that on their side of the table, "fair" looks like this — if they over-negotiate they may push the deal away. Sometimes the right action is *not* to push for more.

This is one of the most common scoring errors — defaulting to "user as victim" framing when the user is actually the powerful party.
